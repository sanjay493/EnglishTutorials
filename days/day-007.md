## Warm-up: 5 minutes

Six days, six tools. You can name things, point at them, say who owns them, and ask questions about them.

Today there is no new grammar. Today you use what you have, find the gaps, and write them down. That list of gaps is the most useful page in your notebook.

**Say it out loud:** "I am Nadia. I live in Bristol. I work for a small company. Do you live here? Where do you work?"

### Today's words

These ten words carry most first conversations. Tick the ones you can use without thinking.

| Word or phrase | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| colleague | a person you work with | My colleague starts at eight. |
| introduce | to tell someone another person's name | Let me introduce my manager. |
| by the way | adds a new topic in casual talk | By the way, do you live nearby? |
| actually | corrects or adds information | Actually, I start at nine, not eight. |

| look forward to | to feel happy about a future event | I look forward to meeting your team. |

**Say it out loud:** cover the middle column and explain each word in your own words. Then use three of them in one sentence each about yourself.

## Grammar: the six forms that matter

### The rule

Everything from Days 1-6 fits into six moves. Say them in this order and check yourself:

1. **Order:** subject, then verb, then the rest. *My sister works in Dubai.*
2. **Be:** *am, is, are*, chosen by the subject. *I am ready. They are here.*
3. **Articles and plurals:** *a* before a consonant sound, *an* before a vowel sound, *the* for something known. *a book, an email, the email.*
4. **Pointing and possession:** *this, that* for one thing; *these, those* for more; *my, your, his, her*; and *'s*. *This is Sara's desk.*
5. **Habits:** the present simple with *-s* in the third person. *He checks email first.*
6. **Questions and negatives:** *do* and *does* carry the work. *Does he check email first? He doesn't check it at home.*

If a sentence feels wrong, ask which of the six moves is missing.

### How it looks

| Form | Verb *be* | Present simple |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Affirmative | She **is** a designer. | She **designs** furniture. |
| Negative | She **isn't** a manager. | She **doesn't design** buildings. |
| Question | **Is** she a designer? | **Does** she design furniture? |
| Short answer | Yes, she **is**. | Yes, she **does**. |
| Wh-question | Where **is** she? | Where **does** she work? |
| One trap | No *do* with *be*. | *-s* moves to *does*. |

### In real sentences

1. **Is** this your first time in Nairobi? My sister **lives** here, so I visit often.
2. She **isn't** in the office today. She **works** from home on Wednesdays.
3. **Does** your manager **speak** Portuguese? I need someone for the Lisbon call.
4. These **are** my colleague's notes, and **this** is the file I mentioned.

### Common mistakes

| Wrong | Right | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| I am work in a bank. | I **work** in a bank. | *Be* and a main verb do not share a sentence. |
| He work in Delhi. | He **works** in Delhi. | Third person singular needs *-s*. |
| Do you be a teacher? | **Are** you a teacher? | With *be*, move the verb. Never use *do*. |
| She don't live here. | She **doesn't** live here. | *She* takes *doesn't*. |
| This are my books. | **These** are my books. | *This* is singular; *these* is plural. |
| I have 32 years. | I **am** 32 **years old**. | Age uses *be* in English, not *have*. |

### Watch out

The biggest of all: **be** or **do**? Look at the verb you want. If it is *be* (*am, is, are*), move it to the front. If it is any other verb, add *do* or *does* and keep the verb in the base form. Say both versions aloud until the choice stops feeling like a decision.

## Speaking lab: meeting someone for the first time

### Dialogue A: informal

> **Situation:** Nadia meets Ben at a friend's birthday party in Bristol. They have never met.

**Nadia:** Hi — I'm Nadia. Are you a friend of Priya's?

**Ben:** Yeah, we work together. I'm Ben.

**Nadia:** Nice to meet you. What do you do at Priya's company?

**Ben:** I'm a project assistant. Mostly admin, honestly. And you?

**Nadia:** I'm a nurse. I work nights, so I'm usually asleep at this time.

**Ben:** Wow. How do you survive that?

**Nadia:** Coffee, mainly. Sorry — what was your name again?

**Ben:** Ben. No problem, it's loud in here.

**Nadia:** Ben. Got it. Do you live near here?

**Ben:** Ten minutes away, actually. By the way, the cake is excellent.

**Nadia:** Then I'm going to the kitchen. Nice to meet you, Ben.

### Dialogue B: formal

> **Situation:** Ms Chen, a conference organiser, greets Mr Okafor at a registration desk in Singapore.

**Ms Chen:** Good morning. Welcome to the conference. May I have your name, please?

**Mr Okafor:** Good morning. It's Daniel Okafor, from Lagos.

**Ms Chen:** Thank you, Mr Okafor. I'm Lin Chen, and I'm coordinating the sessions today. Is this your first time at this event?

**Mr Okafor:** It is, yes. I was invited by a colleague in the finance team.

**Ms Chen:** I see. Which company do you work for?

**Mr Okafor:** I work for a shipping company. I manage the West Africa accounts.

**Ms Chen:** That is helpful to know. The finance session is at eleven. Does that time suit you?

**Mr Okafor:** It does, thank you. I look forward to it.

**Ms Chen:** Excellent. Please ask me if you need anything. Enjoy the conference, Mr Okafor.

**Mr Okafor:** Thank you very much, Ms Chen.

### What changed?

| Informal | Formal | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hi — I'm Nadia. | Good morning. May I have your name, please? | A self-introduction becomes a request with permission |
| Are you a friend of Priya's? | Is this your first time at this event? | Same friendly purpose, neutral topic |
| What do you do? | Which company do you work for? | A precise question replaces a broad one |
| Yeah. / Ten minutes away, actually. | It is, yes. / It does, thank you. | Full short answers, no *yeah* |
| Sorry — what was your name again? | I'm Lin Chen, and I'm coordinating the sessions. | A repair phrase becomes a clear self-identification |
| Nice to meet you. | Enjoy the conference, Mr Okafor. | A friendly goodbye becomes a formal closing formula |

### Pronunciation and delivery

- **Rise, then fall.** *Are you a FRIEND of Priya's?* ↗ Then *I'm a NURSE.* ↘ A first conversation needs both tunes.
- **Weak *are* and *do*.** *Are* is /ə/ and *do* is /də/ in fast speech. Only the information words stay strong.
- **Link the name to the verb.** *I'm‿Ben*, *I'm‿a nurse*. English joins these sounds; it does not pause between them.
- **Say your own name clearly.** Slow down on it, especially on the phone. It is the one word the other person must catch.

### Your turn

Role-play the first meeting twice, 90 seconds each. Round one: a party. Round two: a conference desk. In each round you must (a) introduce yourself, (b) ask two questions, (c) answer with one short answer and one full sentence, and (d) use one repair phrase. Swap roles with a partner, or record both sides yourself and change your voice.

## Writing lab: a guided paragraph (60 words)

### Model text

> **A short introduction paragraph (60 words, for a course welcome page)**
>
> My name is Amara Diallo and I am a logistics coordinator from Dakar. I work for a small transport company and I manage the West Africa routes. I start at eight and usually finish at six. I speak French, Wolof, and English. I am studying English because I want to lead client meetings next year.

### Why it works

- It answers five questions in order: who, where, what job, what routine, why English.
- Every sentence has a subject and a verb. Nothing is missing.
- It uses *be* and the present simple in the same paragraph, correctly separated.
- It ends with a reason, which gives the reader something to reply to.
- It is 60 words, so it fits any profile box or welcome post.

### Structure you can reuse

```text
1. Name + job:   My name is ___ and I am a ___ from ___.
2. Company:      I work for ___ and I ___.
3. Routine:      I start at ___ and usually finish at ___.
4. Languages:    I speak ___ and ___.
5. Reason:       I am studying English because I want to ___.
```

### Editing checklist

- [ ] Every sentence has a subject and a verb.
- [ ] *Be* sentences and action sentences are separate.
- [ ] Third-person verbs, if any, have *-s*.
- [ ] *a* or *an* is correct before each singular noun.
- [ ] The paragraph is 55-65 words. Count them.
- [ ] The last sentence gives a reason, not a fact.

2. ___ (Do / Are) you a student?
3. He ___ (don't / doesn't) drink coffee.
4. ___ (This / These) are my colleague's files.
5. My brother ___ (have / has) two children.

## Answer key

### Exercise 1

1. **works** — *in a hospital* describes an action, so use the present simple, not *be*.
2. **Are** — *a student* is a noun phrase, so the verb is *be*.
3. **doesn't** — *he* takes *doesn't*.
4. **These** — *files* is plural, so the pointing word is *these*.
5. **has** — *have* is irregular in the third person.

### Exercise 2

1. **I work in a bank.** — *Be* and a main verb cannot share a sentence.
2. **Does she work on Saturdays?** — the *-s* belongs to *does*, so the verb returns to the base form.
3. **He doesn't live here.** — *he* takes *doesn't*.
4. **These are my keys.** — *keys* is plural, so *these* + *are*.
5. **I am 30 years old.** — English uses *be* for age, not *have*.

### Exercise 3

1. **Does she work in Lagos?** — *she* takes *does* and the verb loses its *-s*.
2. **They aren't in the office.** — *be* forms its negative with *not*, no *do*.
3. **What time does he start?** — question word + *does* + subject + base verb.

### Exercise 4

One possible version (61 words):

I speak Portuguese and English. I am studying English because I want to train new staff in English next year.

Yours will differ in content. Check the four things a marker would check: sentence completeness, the *be* / action verb split, third-person *-s*, and the word count.
