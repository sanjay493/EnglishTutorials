# Day 6 · Do You...? Yes, I Do: Questions

> **Module 1 · Foundations** · Level A1 · 45-60 minutes
> **Grammar:** present simple negatives, yes/no questions, wh-questions, short answers
> **Speaking:** personal questions and follow-up questions

## Warm-up: 5 minutes

Yesterday you built sentences: *She works in Lisbon. I start at nine.* Today you turn them around and ask about them.

This is the lesson that turns a monologue into a conversation. One small word — **do** or **does** — does most of the work.

**Say it out loud:** "Do you work here? Does she work here? I don't work here. She doesn't work here."

### Today's words

| Word or phrase | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| what | asks about a thing | What do you do? |
| where | asks about a place | Where do you work? |
| when | asks about a time | When do you start? |
| what time | asks about the clock | What time does the meeting start? |
| who | asks about a person | Who do you report to? |
| why | asks about a reason | Why do you start so early? |
| how | asks about the way | How do you commute? |
| how often | asks about frequency | How often do you travel? |
| which | asks about a choice | Which office do you work in? |
| how many | asks about a number | How many people are in your team? |

**Say it out loud:** read the words twice. Then cover the middle column. Ask one question with each word about a person you know.

## Grammar: present simple questions and negatives

### The rule

To ask a present simple question, put **do** or **does** before the subject. The main verb stays in the **base form** — no *-s*.

- Use **do** with *I, you, we, they*: *Do you work here?*
- Use **does** with *he, she, it*: *Does she work here?*

The *-s* moves from the verb to **does**. You never use both.

For negatives, put **don't** or **doesn't** before the base verb: *I don't work on Fridays. He doesn't work on Fridays.*

For **wh-questions**, put the question word first: *Where do you work?*

For **short answers**, use only *do* or *does*: *Yes, I do.* Not *Yes, I work.*

### How it looks

| Form | Example | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Yes/no question | **Do** you work here? | do + I/you/we/they + base verb |
| Yes/no question | **Does** she work here? | does + he/she/it + base verb |
| Wh-question | **Where do** you work? | question word + do + subject + base verb |
| Wh-question | **What time does** he start? | question word + does + subject + base verb |
| Negative | I **don't** work on Fridays. | don't + base verb |
| Negative | He **doesn't** work on Fridays. | doesn't + base verb, no *-s* |
| Short answer | Yes, I **do**. / No, she **doesn't**. | use do/does, not the main verb |

### In real sentences

1. **Do you have a minute?** I want to ask about the new project.
2. My manager **doesn't** start before ten, so we meet after lunch.
3. **How often does** the team travel to the factory? Twice a month.
4. **Who do** you report to in the Berlin office?

### Common mistakes

| Wrong | Right | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Does she works here? | Does she **work** here? | The *-s* is already in *does*. The verb stays base. |
| Do he live in Lisbon? | **Does** he live in Lisbon? | *He* takes *does*, not *do*. |
| Where you work? | Where **do** you work? | A wh-question still needs do/does. |
| I no work on Fridays. | I **don't** work on Fridays. | English negatives need *don't* or *doesn't*. |
| Yes, I work. | Yes, I **do**. | Short answers copy do/does, not the main verb. |
| He don't start until ten. | He **doesn't** start until ten. | Third person singular takes *doesn't*. |

### Watch out

Questions with **be** work differently. With *be*, you move the verb: *Are you a designer? Is she in the office?* With every other verb, you add *do* or *does*: *Do you design furniture?* Never mix them: *Do you be a designer?* is not English. Ask yourself one question first — is the verb *be* or not?

## Speaking lab: personal questions and follow-up questions

### Dialogue A: informal

> **Situation:** Priya meets Tom, a new colleague, at a shared kitchen in Manchester. They have never spoken before.

**Priya:** Hi, you're new, aren't you? Do you work on the design team?

**Tom:** Yeah, I started on Monday. I'm Tom.

**Priya:** Priya. Nice to meet you. Where do you work from?

**Tom:** Mostly here, but I'm at the Leeds office on Fridays.

**Priya:** Oh, how often do you go up there?

**Tom:** Once a week. My team is based there.

**Priya:** That's a long trip. Do you drive?

**Tom:** No, I take the train. It's only an hour.

**Priya:** Sorry, what was your name again?

**Tom:** Tom. And yours?

**Priya:** Priya. I'll forget it by Friday, I promise.

### Dialogue B: formal

> **Situation:** Ms Lund, an HR manager, meets Mr Achebe on his first day at a London office. She is completing his induction record.

**Ms Lund:** Good morning, Mr Achebe. May I ask a few questions for your induction record?

**Mr Achebe:** Of course. Please go ahead.

**Ms Lund:** Thank you. Do you work from this office every day?

**Mr Achebe:** I do, apart from Wednesdays, when I work from home.

**Ms Lund:** I see. And which team do you report to?

**Mr Achebe:** I report to the operations team, under Ms Ferreira.

**Ms Lund:** Thank you. One more question, if you don't mind: does your role involve travel?

**Mr Achebe:** It does. I usually visit the Dublin office once a month.

**Ms Lund:** That is helpful. I will add that to your record. Welcome to the company.

**Mr Achebe:** Thank you very much, Ms Lund.

### What changed?

| Informal | Formal | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hi, you're new, aren't you? | May I ask a few questions for your induction record? | A question about permission, with a stated reason |
| Where do you work from? | Which team do you report to? | A precise work question instead of a general one |
| Oh, how often do you go up there? | Does your role involve travel? | Neutral wording, no informal *up there* |
| Sorry, what was your name again? | I will add that to your record. | A repair phrase replaced by a stated next step |
| Yeah. / No, I take the train. | I do. / It does. | Full short answers instead of one-word replies |
| I'll forget it by Friday! | Welcome to the company. | A joke becomes a formal closing formula |

### Pronunciation and delivery

- **Weak *do*.** In *Do you work here?*, *do* is /də/, not /duː/. Say it quickly and let *you* carry the beat.
- ***Do you* often sounds like "d'you"** in fast casual speech: /dʒə/. That is normal, not lazy. In a formal meeting, slow it down.
- **Yes/no questions rise at the end:** *Do you WORK here?* ↗ Your voice goes up.
- **Wh-questions fall at the end:** *Where do you WORK?* ↘ Your voice goes down.
- **Stress the information word.** *WHERE do you work?* and *Where do you WORK?* ask different things. Choose the one you mean.

### Your turn

Role-play a first conversation. You are the new person. Ask five questions about your partner's work, then ask **two follow-ups** using *how often* and *why*. Use *Does...?* at least once. Record 90 seconds and count your *do/does* questions.

## Writing lab: a first email to a colleague

### Model text

> **Subject:** Hello from the new starter in design
>
> Hi Marta,
>
> My name is Dev, and I join the design team on Monday. I work from the Lisbon office two days a week.
>
> I have three questions before I start. Do you run the Monday team meeting? What time does it usually start? And does the team share files in one folder?
>
> Thank you. I look forward to meeting you.
>
> Best wishes,
> Dev

### Why it works

- The **subject line** says who the sender is and why the email exists, in eight words.
- The first paragraph gives two facts only: who I am, and where I work.
- All three questions sit together in one short paragraph, so Marta can answer them in one reply.
- Every question uses **do/does** or a question word plus **does**. Not one question is missing its helper verb.
- The register is **neutral**: *Hi Marta*, *Best wishes*, first name, no slang.

### Structure you can reuse

```text
1. Subject:  Hello from ___ / Introduction: ___
2. Greeting: Hi ___,
3. Who:      My name is ___ and I ___.
4. Reason:   I have ___ questions before I start.
5. Ask:      Do you ___? What time does ___? And does ___?
6. Close:    Thank you. I look forward to ___.
7. Sign:     Best wishes, ___
```

### Editing checklist

- [ ] The subject line says who you are and what you need.
- [ ] Every question has *do*, *does*, or a question word plus *do/does*.
- [ ] No verb takes *-s* after *does*: *does the team share*, not *does the team shares*.
- [ ] The register is neutral or formal, not casual: *Hi Marta*, not *Hey Marta*.
- [ ] You ask no more than three questions, and each one is a separate sentence.
- [ ] Your name and contact details are at the bottom.

## Answer key

### Exercise 1

1. **Do** — the subject is *you*, so the helper verb is *do*.
2. **Does** — *she* is third person singular, so *does*.
3. **don't** — *I* takes *don't*.
4. **doesn't** — *he* takes *doesn't*.
5. **Do** — *they* takes *do*.

### Exercise 2

1. **When** (or *What time*) — the answer "at six" gives a time.
2. **Where** — the answer gives a place.
3. **Who** — the answer names a person.
4. **How** — the answer "twice a month" gives frequency, and the sentence already has *often*.
5. **Why** — the answer begins with *because*, so the question asks for a reason.

### Exercise 3

1. **Does she work in Lagos?** — *she* takes *does*, and the verb loses its *-s*.
2. **They don't have a meeting on Monday.** — *they* takes *don't* and the base verb *have*.
3. **What time does he start?** — question word + *does* + subject + base verb. *Starts* becomes *start*.

### Exercise 4

One possible version:

> **Subject:** Hello from the new analyst
>
> Hi Ravi,
>
> My name is Leila and I join the finance team next week. I will be in the Dubai office on Tuesdays and Thursdays.
>
> I have three questions. Do you prepare the weekly numbers? What time does the team meeting start? And does the team use one shared sheet?
>
> Thank you. I look forward to meeting you.
>
> Best wishes,
> Leila

Yours can ask different questions. Check only that each one has *do* or *does*, and that no verb after *does* ends in *-s*.
