# Day 1 · Say It Simply: Word Order and the Verb Be

> **Module 1 · Foundations** · Level A1 · 45-60 minutes

> **Speaking:** greetings, introductions, and saying goodbye

## Warm-up: 5 minutes

Welcome. You already know more English than you think. Today you will learn the two things every English sentence needs: a **subject** and a **verb**, in that order.

English is strict about order. "Maya is a writer" is correct. "Is Maya a writer?" is a different sentence: a question. Change the order, change the meaning.

**Say it out loud:** "I am here. You are here. We are here."

### Today's words

| Word or phrase | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| hello / hi | a greeting | Hello, I'm Maya. |
| good morning | a greeting before midday | Good morning, Mr Bell. |
| name | what people call you | My name is Jon. |
| from | the place you come from | I'm from Pune. |
| job | the work you do | My job is interesting. |
| writer | a person who writes | She's a writer. |
| engineer | a person who designs or builds things | He's an engineer. |
| manager | a person who leads a team | I'm the manager here. |
| new | not old; recent | Are you new here? |
| nice to meet you | a friendly first meeting | Nice to meet you, Maya. |

**Say it out loud:** read the words twice. Then cover the middle column and explain each word in your own words.

## Grammar: word order and the verb *be*

### The rule

An English statement usually follows **subject + verb + object or complement**. The subject is the person or thing doing or being something. The verb tells us what they do or what they are. The object or complement completes the idea.

The verb **be** has three present forms: **am**, **is**, **are**. Use **am** with *I*, **is** with *he / she / it*, and **are** with *you / we / they*. In speech, we usually contract: *I'm*, *you're*, *he's*, *she's*, *it's*, *we're*, *they're*.

### How it looks

| Subject | Affirmative | Negative | Question |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| I | I'm a writer. | I'm not a teacher. | Am I late? |
| you / we / they | You're early. | They aren't here. | Are you new? |
| he / she / it | She's a doctor. | He isn't busy. | Is it your first day? |

Notice three things:

1. The negative is **not** before the noun: *I'm not ready*, not *I not am ready*.
2. In questions, **be** moves before the subject: *Are you new?*
3. English needs a subject. Use **it** for weather and time: *It's cold. It's Monday.* Never *Is cold.*

### In real sentences

1. **I'm Maya.** I'm a writer from Pune, and I'm new here.
2. **She's the manager.** She isn't in the office today.
3. **Are you from the Leeds office?** No, I'm not. I'm from Manchester.

### Common mistakes

| Wrong | Right | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| I am agree. | I agree. | *Agree* is a verb, not an adjective. Do not add *am*. |
| She have 25 years. | She is 25. | Age uses *be*, not *have*. |
| He is work in Delhi. | He works in Delhi. | One verb per idea. *Work* is already the verb. |
| My name Sanjay. | My name is Sanjay. | The verb *is* cannot be left out. |
| I'm boring. | I'm bored. | *Boring* describes the thing; *bored* describes your feeling. |

### Watch out

**Be** describes states: identity, age, feelings, nationality, jobs. **Do** describes actions: *I work*, *I live*, *I study*. That is why you say *I'm a designer* but *I design websites*. Same person, two different verbs.

## Speaking lab: greetings and introductions

### Dialogue A: informal

> **Situation:** Monday morning in the kitchen of a co-working space. Maya and Jon meet for the first time.

**Maya:** Hi, I'm Maya. Are you new here?

**Jon:** Yeah, I'm Jon. Nice to meet you. I'm here for the design team.

**Maya:** Oh, nice. Where are you from?

**Jon:** I'm from Leeds, but I live in Manchester now. And you?

**Maya:** I'm from Pune. I'm a writer.

**Jon:** Cool. Is it your first day?

**Maya:** It is. I'm a bit nervous, actually.

**Jon:** You're fine. The coffee is terrible, but the people are nice.

### Dialogue B: formal

> **Situation:** the registration desk at a conference. Ms Rao checks in Mr Bell.

**Ms Rao:** Good morning. Are you here for the marketing summit?

**Mr Bell:** Yes, I am. My name is Jonathan Bell. I'm from the Leeds office.

**Ms Rao:** Welcome, Mr Bell. Is this your first summit?

**Mr Bell:** It is, yes. I'm afraid I don't have my badge yet.

**Ms Rao:** That's no problem. Here you are. Your seat is in Hall B.

**Mr Bell:** Thank you very much.

**Ms Rao:** You're welcome. Enjoy the summit.

### What changed?

| Informal | Formal | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hi | Good morning | Time of day and a more formal greeting |
| I'm Maya / I'm Jon | My name is Jonathan Bell | Full name, no contraction |
| Yeah | Yes | Standard form in formal speech |
| Is it your first day? | Is this your first summit? | More precise noun; *this* is slightly more formal than *it* here |
| Thanks / You're fine | Thank you very much / You're welcome | Complete politeness formulas |

### Pronunciation and delivery

- **Contractions are one sound.** *I'm* is /aɪm/, not "I am" said quickly. *You're* sounds like *yaw* in British English and *yur* in American English.
- **Stress the new information.** *I'm from PUNE.* Stress goes on the place, not on *from*.
- **Weak "is".** In *She's a teacher*, the *'s* is very light: /ʃiz ə ˈtiːtʃə/.
- **Rise, then fall.** *Are you NEW here?* rises at the end. *Nice to MEET you.* falls.

### Your turn

You are at a training day. Introduce yourself to one person, informally, in 30 seconds. Use **I'm**, **I'm from**, and **I'm a/an**. Then do it again as if you are at a formal registration desk. Record both.

## Writing lab: a personal information form and a three-sentence bio

### Model text

**Personal information form**

| Field | Your answer |
| --- | --- |
| First name | Maya |
| Surname | Kulkarni |
| Nationality | Indian |
| Job | Writer |
| City | Pune |
| Email | maya@example.com |

**Three-sentence bio**

> Hello. My name is Maya Kulkarni. I am a writer from Pune, India. I am new here and I am happy to meet you.

### Why it works

- It has exactly three sentences, all with a subject and a verb.
- It moves from **greeting** to **identity** to **feeling**, which is the natural order of an introduction.
- It uses no contractions in the formal version, which makes it slightly more careful.
- It gives one detail (the city) that invites a follow-up question.

### Structure you can reuse

```text
1. Greeting:        Hello. / Good morning.
2. Identity:        My name is ___ . I am a ___ from ___ .
3. Situation:       I am here for ___ . / I am new here.
```

### Editing checklist

- [ ] Every sentence has a subject and a verb.
- [ ] *I am* or *I'm* is correct for the register: full form for a form or formal bio, contraction for a message.
- [ ] Capitals on names, countries, cities, and nationalities.
- [ ] No sentence starts with *Is* unless it is a question.

## Answer key

### Exercise 1

1. **am** — with *I*, the form is always *am*.
2. **is** — with *she*, use *is*.
3. **are** — with *they*, use *are*.
4. **are** — with *you*, use *are*, even for one person.
5. **is** — with *he*, use *is*.

### Exercise 2

1. **is** — *My name* is singular.
2. **am / am** — *I* takes *am*; the second clause is also about *I*.
3. **are** — *we* takes *are*.
4. **is** — *it* takes *is*.
5. **aren't** — *they* takes *are*; the negative is *aren't*.

### Exercise 3

1. **I agree with you.** — *Agree* is the verb. No *am*.
2. **She is 25.** — Age uses *be*.
3. **It is raining today.** — English needs the subject *it*.

### Exercise 4

One possible formal version: *Good morning. My name is Sanjay Mehta. I am a project manager from Mumbai, India. I am here for the leadership workshop.*

One possible informal version: *Hi, I'm Sanjay. I'm a project manager from Mumbai. I'm here for the workshop.*
