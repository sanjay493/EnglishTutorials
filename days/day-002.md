## Warm-up: 5 minutes

Yesterday you learned *I am Maya* and *She is a writer*. Today you will stop repeating names. Instead of *Maya is a writer. Maya is from Pune. Maya is new*, you will say: *Maya is a writer. **She** is from Pune. **She** is new.*

**Say it out loud:** "I am here. You are here. He is here. She is here. We are here. They are here."

## Grammar: subject pronouns and *be* questions

### The rule

**Subject pronouns** replace the name of the person or thing that does the action: *I, you, he, she, it, we, they*.

- Use **he** for a man or boy, **she** for a woman or girl, **it** for a thing, place, or idea.
- Use **they** for two or more people or things. **They** is also the standard pronoun for one person when you do not know or do not want to state gender: *Someone called. They didn't leave a name.*
- English needs the subject every time. *Is my brother* is not a sentence; *He is my brother* is.

### How it looks

| Person | Pronoun | Affirmative | Negative | Question |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1st singular | I | I'm ready. | I'm not ready. | Am I late? |
| 2nd | you | You're early. | You aren't early. | Are you new? |
| 3rd singular | he | He's here. | He isn't here. | Is he here? |
| 3rd singular | she | She's a manager. | She isn't a manager. | Is she a manager? |
| 3rd singular | it | It's Monday. | It isn't Monday. | Is it Monday? |
| 1st plural | we | We're a team. | We aren't a team. | Are we ready? |
| 3rd plural | they | They're colleagues. | They aren't colleagues. | Are they colleagues? |

### Short answers

| Question | Yes | No |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Are you new? | Yes, I **am**. | No, I'm **not**. |
| Is she a manager? | Yes, she **is**. | No, she **isn't**. |
| Are they here? | Yes, they **are**. | No, they **aren't**. |
| Is it Monday? | Yes, it **is**. | No, it **isn't**. |

In a positive short answer, use the **full form**: *Yes, I am*, not *Yes, I'm*. In a negative short answer, the contraction is normal: *No, I'm not*.

### Introducing other people

- Informal: *This is Ravi. He's my cousin.*
- Formal: *May I introduce Ana Silva? She's our new project lead.*

Use **this is** for one person, **these are** for two or more. (More on *this/these* tomorrow.)

### In real sentences

1. **This is my sister, Meera.** **She's** a doctor in Delhi.
2. **Are they** on your team? **Yes, they are.** **They're** both engineers.
3. **It's** my first day, so **I'm** not sure where the meeting room is.

### Common mistakes

| Wrong | Right | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Me am a student. | I am a student. | *Me* is an object pronoun. The subject is *I*. |
| Him is my brother. | He is my brother. | Use *he* for the subject. |
| My father he is a doctor. | My father is a doctor. | Do not use a noun and a pronoun as a double subject. |
| Yes, I'm. | Yes, I am. | Positive short answers use the full form. |
| No, I amn't. | No, I'm not. | There is no *amn't* in standard English. |
| Are you a teacher? Yes, I are. | Yes, I am. | The answer must match the pronoun. |

### Watch out

*They* is not only plural. It is also correct for one person of unknown gender: *The new designer starts today. **They** are based in Lisbon.* This is standard modern English, not slang.

## Speaking lab: introducing yourself and other people

### Dialogue A: informal

> **Situation:** a friend's birthday party. Priya introduces her cousin Ravi to her friend Sam.

**Priya:** Sam, this is my cousin Ravi. Ravi, this is Sam.

**Sam:** Hi, Ravi. Nice to meet you.

**Ravi:** Nice to meet you too. Are you from Bristol?

**Sam:** No, I'm not. I'm from Bath, but I work in Bristol now.

**Ravi:** Oh, me too. Well, near Bristol.

**Priya:** They're both from the west country. I'm the odd one out.

**Sam:** Are you here for the whole weekend?

**Ravi:** No, I'm not. I'm going back on Sunday.

### Dialogue B: formal

> **Situation:** a client meeting. Ms Ito introduces her new colleague to Mr Clark.

**Ms Ito:** Mr Clark, may I introduce Ana Silva? She's our new project lead.

**Mr Clark:** How do you do, Ms Silva.

**Ana:** How do you do. It's a pleasure to meet you.

**Mr Clark:** Are you based in the Lisbon office?

**Ana:** Yes, I am. I joined last month.

**Mr Clark:** And are you leading the Meridian account?

**Ana:** I am, yes. Ms Ito is supporting me during the transition.

**Ms Ito:** Ana is the lead. I'm here for the first two weeks.

### What changed?

| Informal | Formal | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| This is Ravi. | May I introduce Ana Silva? | A formal introduction asks permission |
| Hi | How do you do | A fixed formal greeting; the reply is the same phrase |
| Are you from Bristol? | Are you based in the Lisbon office? | *Based in* is the workplace equivalent of *from* |
| No, I'm not. | I am, yes. | Full form, and a warmer confirmation |
| He's my cousin. | She's our new project lead. | Role and title instead of family relationship |

### Pronunciation and delivery

- **Weak *are*.** In *Are you new?*, *are* is usually /ə/, almost part of the next word: *ə jə NU?*
- **Linking.** *This is* sounds like one word: /ðɪsɪz/. *He is* becomes /hiz/.
- **Pronoun stress.** Stress the pronoun only to correct someone: ***SHE'S** the manager, not me.*
- **Intonation in introductions.** *Nice to MEET you* falls. *Are you HERE?* rises.

### Your turn

Introduce two people to each other. First at a party, then at a client meeting. In the formal version, use **may I introduce**, a title and surname, and one sentence about each person's role. Record it.

## Writing lab: short chat messages

### Model text

**Informal message (chat)**

> Hi Sam! Quick intro: this is Ravi, my cousin. He's a photographer and he's new in town. You two should talk about the Bristol shoot. 🙂

**Formal message (email or LinkedIn)**

> Dear Mr Clark,
>
> I'd like to introduce Ana Silva, our new project lead. She is based in Lisbon and will manage the Meridian account.
>
> I'm copying Ana here so you can arrange a call.
>
> Best regards,
> Kenji Ito

### Why it works

- **Purpose in the first line.** The reader knows why the message exists before reading the details.
- **One sentence per person.** Name, role, and one useful fact. Nothing else.
- **A clear next step.** "You two should talk" and "you can arrange a call" tell the reader what to do.
- **Register matches the channel.** Contractions and an emoji in chat; full forms and a sign-off in email.

### Structure you can reuse

```text
1. Greeting:      Hi ___ / Dear ___,
2. Purpose:       Quick intro: this is ___ / I'd like to introduce ___
3. Who + role:    He's/She's a ___ and ___
4. Next step:     You two should ___ / I'm copying ___ so you can ___
5. Sign-off:      (chat: none) / Best regards, ___
```

### Editing checklist

- [ ] Every pronoun clearly points to one person.
- [ ] *He* / *she* matches the person; *they* for a group or unknown gender.
- [ ] Contractions only where the channel allows them.
- [ ] Names, titles, and company names are spelled correctly.
- [ ] There is one clear next step.

## Answer key

### Exercise 1

1. **She** — one woman, subject position.
2. **We** — the speaker plus another person.
3. **It** — a thing.
4. **You** — the person spoken to, even with more than one person.
5. **He** — one man.

### Exercise 2

1. **am** — *you* in the question becomes *I* in the answer.
2. **isn't** — negative answer for *she*.
3. **are** — positive answer keeps the full form.
4. **isn't** — negative answer for *it*.
5. **aren't** — negative answer for *we*.

### Exercise 3

1. **I am a designer.** — *Me* cannot be a subject.
2. **He is my colleague.** — *Him* is an object pronoun.
3. **My mother is a teacher.** — No double subject.
4. **Yes, I am.** — Positive short answers take the full form.
