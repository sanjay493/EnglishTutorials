# Day 9 · Some Coffee, Any Sugar? Quantity

## Warm-up: 5 minutes

Yesterday you said *There are two chairs*. Chairs are easy: you can count them. Bread is not. You do not say *two breads*. You say *two loaves of bread*.

Today we look at the difference between **things you can count** and **things you cannot count**, and the small words that go with them: *some, any, much, many*.

**Say it out loud:** "Some bread, some eggs. Any milk? Any coffee? How much rice? How many eggs?"

| Word or phrase | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| bread | a basic food made from flour | There isn't much bread left. |
| rice | small grains, cooked in water | We need a kilo of rice. |
| milk | a white drink from cows or plants | Is there any milk in the fridge? |
| sugar | a sweet white powder | How much sugar do you take? |

| a loaf of bread | one whole bread, sold in one piece | Can I have a loaf of bread? |

## Grammar: countable and uncountable nouns

### The rule

Some nouns you can count: *one egg, two eggs, three eggs*. These are **countable**. Some nouns you cannot count: *water, rice, sugar, money, information*. These are **uncountable**. You never put a number directly before them.

Uncountable nouns take a singular verb: *The rice is ready.* To give them a number, count the measure: *a bottle of water, two kilos of rice, three loaves of bread*.

Use **some** in positive sentences and in offers: *I need some sugar. Would you like some coffee?* Use **any** in negatives and most questions: *We don't have any milk. Is there any bread?* Use **much** with uncountable nouns and **many** with countable nouns.

### How it looks

| Form | Countable | Uncountable | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| some (+) | some eggs | some rice | also in offers: *Would you like some tea?* |
| any (− and ?) | any eggs | any milk | negative sentences and most questions |
| much | not used | much time | mostly negatives and questions |
| many | many eggs | not used | all sentence types |
| How much / How many | How many eggs? | How much rice? | *How much* also asks a price |
| a lot of | a lot of eggs | a lot of rice | best choice in positive sentences |

### Counting the uncountable

| Measure word | Example |
| --- | --- |
| a bottle of | a bottle of water |
| a cup of | a cup of coffee |
| a kilo of | a kilo of rice |
| a loaf of | a loaf of bread |
| a carton of | a carton of milk |
| a piece of | a piece of cheese |

### In real sentences

1. We have some bread, but we don't have any eggs.
2. How much rice do we need? About a kilo, I think.
3. How many people are coming? There aren't many chairs, so six at most.

### Common mistakes

| Wrong | Right | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| I need some informations. | I need some **information**. | *Information* is uncountable. It has no plural. |
| How many money do you have? | How **much** money do you have? | *Money* is uncountable, so it takes *much*. |
| I don't have some milk. | I don't have **any** milk. | Negative sentences take *any*. |
| There are many water in the bottle. | There is **a lot of** water in the bottle. | *Water* is uncountable, so the verb is *is*. |
| Can I have an advice? | Can I have **some advice**? | *Advice* is uncountable: *a piece of advice*. |
| I'd like two breads. | I'd like **two loaves of bread**. | Count the measure, not the food. |

### Watch out

In offers and requests we use **some**, even in a question: *Would you like some coffee? Could I have some water?* Here the speaker expects a *yes*. Use **any** when you really do not know: *Is there any milk?* Both are correct questions; the difference is what you expect.

## Speaking lab: ordering food and drink

### Dialogue A: informal

> **Situation:** Priya and Dan are at a café counter in Manchester, ordering lunch.

**Priya:** I'm starving. What are you having?

**Dan:** The soup, maybe. Is there any bread with it?

**Priya:** I don't know. Ask.

**Dan:** Can I get the soup and some bread, please?

**Priya:** I'll have a cheese sandwich. And a coffee.

**Dan:** How much is that?

**Priya:** No idea. Do you have any cash?

**Dan:** About ten pounds. Sorry, what did you say you wanted?

**Priya:** A cheese sandwich and a coffee. No sugar.

**Dan:** Got it. Two minutes.

### Dialogue B: formal

> **Situation:** Mr Okafor and Ms Lindqvist are at a restaurant in Leeds, at a business lunch. Tomas is their waiter.

**Tomas:** Good afternoon. Are you ready to order?

**Mr Okafor:** Almost. Could I ask what the soup of the day is?

**Tomas:** It's tomato and basil, sir.

**Mr Okafor:** Thank you. I think I'll have the soup, then the chicken.

**Ms Lindqvist:** And I'd like the fish, without potatoes, please.

**Tomas:** Certainly. Would you like some bread while you wait?

**Ms Lindqvist:** Yes, please. Just a little.

**Tomas:** And to drink?

**Mr Okafor:** Still water, please. A bottle for the table.

**Ms Lindqvist:** The same for me.

**Tomas:** Very good. Would you like anything else?

**Mr Okafor:** Not for now, thank you.

### What changed?

| Informal | Formal | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What are you having? | Are you ready to order? | A friend asks casually; the server asks politely |
| Can I get the soup...? | I think I'll have the soup... | *Can I get* is casual; *I think I'll have* sounds softer |
| A cheese sandwich and a coffee. | And I'd like the fish, without potatoes, please. | A fragment becomes a full sentence with *would like* |
| No sugar. | Just a little. / without potatoes | A short negative becomes a polite request |
| Do you have any cash? | A bottle for the table, please. | A personal question vs a clear request to the server |
| Got it. | Very good. | Casual confirmation vs formal acknowledgement |

### Pronunciation and delivery

- **some** weakens in fast speech: /səm/. *Some bread* sounds like "s'm bread". Before a vowel it stays stronger: *some eggs*.
- **any** has stress on the first syllable: **E**-ny.
- **Stress the quantity word.** *How MUCH rice?* *How MANY eggs?* The voice lands on *much* and *many*.
- **Offers rise at the end:** *Would you like some COFfee?* ↗

## Writing lab: a shopping list and a short note

### Model text

> **Shopping list — Saturday market**
>
> - a loaf of bread
> - two kilos of rice
> - six eggs
> - some coffee
> - a bottle of olive oil
> - four tomatoes
> - a little cheese
>
> **Note to Sam**
>
> Hi Sam,
>
> I'm going to the market this morning. We need bread and rice, but there isn't much coffee left, so please add some to the list. There aren't any eggs in the fridge, so I'll get six. Could you buy a bottle of olive oil on your way home? We don't have any.
>
> Thanks,
> Ana

### Why it works

- The list uses no full sentences, and the quantity comes before the item: *two kilos of rice*.
- The note explains **why** each item is needed: *there isn't much coffee left*, *there aren't any eggs*.
- It uses *some*, *any*, *much* and measure words in one short text, so it practises the whole lesson.
- It ends with one clear request, so the reader knows exactly what to do.

### Structure you can reuse

```text
1. Heading:   Shopping list — ___ (shop or day)
2. Items:     quantity + item (a loaf of, two kilos of, some, a little)
3. Greeting:  Hi ___, 
4. Why:       We need ___, but there isn't much ___. There aren't any ___.
5. Request:   Could you buy ___ on your way home?
6. Closing:   Thanks, ___
```

### Editing checklist

- [ ] No number directly before an uncountable noun: *two kilos of rice*, not *two rices*.
- [ ] *Some* in positive sentences, *any* in negatives and questions.
- [ ] *Much* with uncountable nouns, *many* with countable nouns.
- [ ] The note says why the person should buy each thing.
- [ ] The register is right: this is a note to someone you live with, so *Hi Sam* is correct, not *Dear Sir*.

## Answer key

### Exercise 1

1. **some** — a positive sentence about something you need.
2. **any** — a negative sentence.
3. **some** — an offer; the speaker expects *yes*.
4. **any** — a real question; you do not know the answer.
5. **any** — a negative sentence.

### Exercise 2

1. **much** — *sugar* is uncountable.
2. **many** — *people* is countable.
3. **many** — *chairs* is countable.
4. **much** — *time* is uncountable.
5. **much** — *rice* is uncountable.

### Exercise 3

1. **I need two loaves of bread.** — count the measure, not the food.
2. **There is a lot of water in the bottle.** — *water* is uncountable, so the verb is *is*, and *a lot of* is natural in a positive sentence.
3. **I don't have any cheese.** — negative sentences take *any*.
