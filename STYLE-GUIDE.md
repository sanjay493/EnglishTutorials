# Style guide: how every lesson is written

This is the house style for English 120. It exists so that 120 lessons written over months, by different runs of the lesson writer, still read like one course.

If a rule here conflicts with a rule in a source, this guide wins for *our* pages. Sources are credited, never copied.

## 1. Who we are writing for

- An ambitious adult learner, A1 at the start, who wants English for work and travel, not just for exams.
- Someone studying alone, 45-60 minutes a day, often on a phone.
- Someone who may be a speaker of Indian English, or any other variety. Never imply that one accent is "correct" and another is "wrong". Teach **appropriacy**, not superiority.

Write to the learner as **you**. Use **we** only when the course itself is speaking ("In this module we look at...").

## 2. The voice

| Do | Don't |
| --- | --- |
| Explain the rule in one clear paragraph | Stack three exceptions before the basic rule |
| Use concrete, adult examples (work, travel, money, family, health) | Use childish example sentences |
| Name the mistake and say why it happens | Say "this is wrong" without a reason |
| Use short sentences, then one longer sentence to show range | Write long, nested sentences to sound academic |
| Keep encouragement specific ("If you can do X, you are ready for Day 8") | Use empty praise ("Great job!") |
| Be direct about difficulty ("This is the hardest tense for most learners") | Pretend everything is easy |

Humanness test: read the opening paragraph aloud. If it sounds like a textbook caption, rewrite it.

## 3. Level calibration

The level is a ceiling, not a target. A B1 lesson can use a B2 word if it is defined; an A1 lesson cannot use a B1 structure without explanation.

| Level | Max sentence length | Tense range | New words per lesson | Target lesson length |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A1 | 12 words | present, `be`, `can`, basic past | 8 | 750-900 words |
| A2 | 16 words | past, future, present perfect (intro) | 10 | 800-950 words |
| B1 | 20 words | all simple tenses, first/second conditional, passive | 12 | 900-1,050 words |
| B2 | 25 words | perfect tenses, third conditional, reported speech | 14 | 950-1,100 words |
| C1 | 30 words | full range, inversion, nominalisation | 16 | 1,000-1,200 words |
| C2 | No limit, but clarity first | full range, marked structures | 16 | 1,000-1,200 words |

At every level: **one** new grammar focus per lesson. Review structures may appear, but they are not explained again in full.

## 4. Page anatomy

Every lesson uses the headings from `TUTORIAL-TEMPLATE.md`, in this order:

1. `## Warm-up: 5 minutes` - bridge from yesterday, plus today's words table.
2. `## Grammar: <topic>` - rule, table, examples, common mistakes, contrast.
3. `## Speaking lab: <topic>` - informal dialogue, formal dialogue, what changed, pronunciation, your turn.
4. `## Writing lab: <topic>` - model text, why it works, reusable structure, editing checklist.
5. `## Practice: 10 minutes` - four exercises, increasing difficulty.
6. `## Answer key` - answers with reasons.
7. `## Check yourself` - can-do table and score.
8. `## Tomorrow` - one-sentence preview.
9. `## Go deeper` - real sources and one self-study task.

Do not add new top-level sections without updating the template and this guide.

## 5. Grammar explanations

A grammar section must contain, in this order:

1. **The rule** in plain language, one paragraph.
2. **A form table** showing affirmative, negative, question.
3. **At least three real sentences** with context, not isolated fragments.
4. **A common mistakes table** with `Wrong | Right | Why`.
5. **A contrast** with the structure learners confuse it with.

Rules of thumb:

- Describe **use before form** when the meaning is the hard part ("We use the present perfect when the time is not finished or not stated").
- Give the **frequency** where it matters: "In speech, *gonna* is common in American English and informal British English; do not write it in an email."
- Mark **register** explicitly: formal, neutral, informal, slang.
- If a rule has a well-known exception, put it in a short "Watch out" line, not in the main rule.

## 6. Dialogues and speaking labs

Every speaking lab has **two dialogues with the same communicative purpose**: one informal, one formal. That contrast is the lesson.

Dialogue requirements:

- A named situation and named speakers. No "A:" and "B:".
- 8-12 turns for A1-B1; 10-16 for B2+.
- Natural spoken features: contractions, short answers, fillers (`well`, `actually`, `I mean`), and one **repair phrase** (`Sorry, could you say that again?`).
- The formal version changes at least four things: title/surname, full forms, indirect questions, hedging or politeness markers, and the closing.
- A `What changed?` table that makes the register shift visible.

Pronunciation notes use CAPITALS for stress (`imPORtant`), `/slashes/` for a rough sound, and a plain-language description ("the *t* almost disappears"). Do not require the learner to read full IPA unless the lesson is about phonetics.

## 7. Writing labs

The model text must be:

- **Realistic**: something a person would actually send or publish.
- **Labeled**: email, notice, report, essay, post, minutes, proposal, and so on.
- **Audience-aware**: say who reads it and what they need from it.
- **Short**: 60-120 words for A1-B1, 120-250 for B2, 250-350 for C1.

After the model, give a numbered **reusable structure** and a **checklist** with at least four items, including one register item and one accuracy item.

Never present a model as the only correct answer. In the answer key, write "One possible version" for productive tasks.

## 8. Exercises and answer keys

- Four exercises: recognition, controlled production, transformation, free production.
- Five items for Exercises 1-2, three for Exercise 3, one for Exercise 4.
- Answer keys give the answer **and the reason**. For transformations, show the full sentence.
- Distractors must be plausible errors, not random words.
- Difficulty rises within the exercise, not across it.

## 9. British, American, and global English

Default to **British spelling** in the lesson prose, with the American form in brackets on first use when it differs: `colour (color)`, `organise (organize)`, `centre (center)`, `travelling (traveling)`.

- Quotes and proper names keep their original spelling.
- In dialogues, the variety should match the setting. Say where the speakers are.
- Never mark a variant as a mistake. Mark it as a **variety** or a **register** choice.
- Use `-ise` for British verbs, but note that Oxford spelling uses `-ize`.
- When a word has different meanings across varieties (`rubber`, `boot`, `braces`, `first floor`), flag the misunderstanding explicitly.

## 10. Formality scale

Use this five-point scale consistently in labels and tables:

| Label | Typical use | Markers |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Intimate | family, close friends | slang, ellipsis, shared references |
| Casual | colleagues you know, peers | contractions, phrasal verbs, `yeah` |
| Neutral | most workplace writing, strangers | standard grammar, light contractions |
| Formal | clients, senior management, official letters | full forms, passive, hedging, titles |
| Frozen | legal, ceremonial, published policy | fixed formulas, no contractions |

## 11. Accessibility and inclusion

- Avoid idioms as the only way to express a key idea; define them.
- Use gender-neutral examples (`they` for a single unknown person is standard modern English).
- Include names and settings from several regions, not only the UK and the US.
- Describe disabilities and accents neutrally and only when relevant.
- Keep tables narrow enough to read on a phone (max 5 columns).
- Never use colour alone to convey meaning.

## 12. Accuracy and sources

- Do not invent statistics, studies, quotations, or "research shows".
- Prefer sources from `SOURCES.md`. If you use a new source, add it there with a working URL.
- Paraphrase. Do not copy more than a short phrase, and put quotation marks around it.
- If two respected sources disagree (for example, on the Oxford comma), say so and recommend a house choice.

## 13. Definition of done

A lesson may move from `status: draft` to `status: complete` only when all of these are true:

- [ ] Front matter matches `ops/curriculum.json` (day, module, level, topics).
- [ ] All nine headings from the template are present.
- [ ] Word count is within the range for the level.
- [ ] Grammar section has a form table, 3+ examples, 2+ common mistakes, and a contrast.
- [ ] Both dialogues exist and differ in register in at least four ways.
- [ ] Writing lab has a model, a reusable structure, and a checklist.
- [ ] Four exercises and a full answer key with reasons.
- [ ] `node scripts/build-all.mjs` passes with no errors.
- [ ] Links resolve, and no placeholder text (`TODO`, `...` as a whole line, `{{...}}`) remains.
- [ ] `words` in the front matter matches the real count (the build script reports it).

## 14. Git conventions

- One lesson per commit: `lesson(day-007): checkpoint 1 - first conversations`.
- Index or tooling changes: `chore(index): rebuild sidebar and progress`.
- Curriculum changes: `plan(module-3): swap day 41 and 42`.
- Never commit a lesson that fails `node scripts/check.mjs`.
